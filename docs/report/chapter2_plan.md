Started 08 October 2026. Every citation in `references.bib` was verified
against a publisher record or an author's own copy; nothing was written from

A literature survey is not a list of summaries. Its job is to make the case
that this project needed building, by showing precisely what prior work does
and where it stops. The structure below is built around that argument, not
around chronology.

---

## The anchor: a peer-reviewed statement of this project's thesis

The single most useful thing found today. Novak, Joy and Kermek's systematic
review of source-code similarity tools in academia concludes:

> tools do not find plagiarism regardless of how good they are

and that humans must judge what counts as plagiarism. They also name JPlag,
MOSS and Sherlock Warwick as the most promising available tools, and report
that most academic tools are never released and never compared against one
another.

**This is CodeGuard's "evidence system, not a verdict machine" principle,
stated in ACM Transactions on Computing Education rather than asserted by the
author of the project.** Chapter 1 should quote it. It converts the design
philosophy from a preference into a position with support, and it pre-empts the
obvious challenge that ranking rather than deciding is a way of dodging the
hard part.

It also supplies the justification for RPS being a *ranked* score with its
evidence attached rather than a percentage with a threshold.

## Strand 1 — Token and fingerprint methods, and what they cannot see

`schleimer2003`, `prechelt2002`, `novak2019`

Winnowing selects the minimum hash from each window of consecutive k-gram
hashes, which guarantees that any shared substring of length at least
`w + k - 1` produces at least one matching fingerprint. That guarantee is why
MOSS works and why it scales. JPlag instead tokenises by language and runs
greedy string tiling over the token streams.

**Where they stop, and this is the whole opening for Layer 2 and 3.** Both
compare submissions *against each other*. They are built to find two students
who handed in the same work. They are structurally incapable of flagging:

- work outsourced to a freelancer, where there is no second copy in the cohort
- work generated by a model, for the same reason
- a student submitting someone else's work from a previous year's cohort,
  unless that corpus is retained and included

A tool that only compares submissions pairwise can only ever detect collusion.
That is a real problem and the most common one, but it is one of three named in
this project's scope, and the other two need a different question asked.

## Strand 2 — Tree and AST methods, which is Layer 1's actual ancestry

`pawlik2016`, `pawlik2015`, `jiang2007`, `zakeri2023`

The move from token streams to trees is what buys resistance to renaming,
reordering and reformatting, because an AST discards exactly the surface
detail those transformations change. Pawlik and Augsten's APTED is the
algorithm Layer 1 calls; their earlier RTED paper is the one that establishes
the dynamic decomposition strategy, and the APTED authors ask that both be
cited. DECKARD takes the alternative route of turning subtrees into
count vectors and clustering them with locality-sensitive hashing, which
trades exactness for scale.

Zakeri-Nasrabadi et al. give the taxonomy to position Layer 1 inside: text,
token, tree, graph, metric, learning, image and test-based, with hybrids the
most common at about 28% of studies. They also report that Type IV (semantic)
clones remain hard and that many techniques simply cannot detect them —
**a limitation Layer 1 shares and the report should admit rather than discover
under questioning.**

Their finding that nearly half of surveyed tools support Java, and that many
languages have little or no support, is also the cleanest justification for
this project measuring both Java and Python rather than claiming portability.

## Strand 3 — Authorship attribution, which is Layer 2's ancestry and its trap

`caliskan2015`, `frantzeskou2006`, `abuhamad2019`

Code stylometry asks "who wrote this" from style alone. Caliskan-Islam et al.
is the canonical result: roughly 94% accuracy over 1,600 programmers and 98%
over 250, using lexical, layout and AST-derived features on Google Code Jam
submissions. Frantzeskou et al.'s SCAP method gets comparable results from
byte-level n-gram profiles without parsing at all. Abuhamad et al. push the
same task to large author sets with deep feature learning.

### The caveat, and it is the most likely hard question in the viva

An examiner who reads Caliskan-Islam will ask why this project reports roughly
**1.4x to 2.5x over chance** when the literature reports **94%**. The answer
must be ready, because it sounds damning and is not:

1. **Different task.** They perform closed-set attribution: given a file and a
   known list of N candidate authors, pick one. Layer 2 performs anomaly
   detection against a single baseline: given a file and this student's own
   earlier work, is it consistent? There is no candidate list, because the
   ghostwriter is not enrolled.
2. **Different feature budget.** They use hundreds to thousands of features,
   including deep AST-derived ones. `behavioural.ts` scores four, because
   four is what survived measurement on real code and because every feature
   must be explainable to a student contesting a flag.
3. **Different data.** Google Code Jam gives many authors solving *identical*
   problems, which holds the task constant and lets style dominate. Coursework
   does the same, which is the fair comparison — but this project's measured
   figures come from library code across 26 repositories, where the project
   itself is a confound, and the honest within-project figures are the lower
   ones.
4. **Different success criterion.** Attribution is graded on picking the right
   author. Layer 2 is graded on whether a human reviewer, shown the evidence,
   reaches a defensible decision. A 2x lift that surfaces the right twenty
   submissions out of five hundred is useful; a 94% attribution accuracy over
   a candidate set that does not contain the real author is meaningless.

**Say this in Chapter 2, not only in Chapter 7.** Positioning the project
against the attribution literature up front is much stronger than defending
the gap after presenting the numbers.

## Strand 4 — AI-generated code detection, and why Layer 3 was dropped

`nguyen2023`, `gptsniffer2024`

GPTSniffer fine-tunes CodeBERT to separate human-written from ChatGPT-written
code and reports beating GPTZero and the OpenAI text classifier. Notably, the
authors find that performance improves when the training data resembles the
target code and when snippets are classified in pairs.

**That last finding is the argument for dropping Layer 3, and it should be
presented as a decision rather than an omission.** A classifier whose accuracy
depends on training data resembling the target needs a labelled corpus of
student work from the course it will police. No such corpus exists here.
Without it the layer could be built but its performance could never be
reported, and an unreportable layer in a system whose entire premise is
auditable evidence is worse than an absent one. Chapter 8 lists it as future
work with the corpus as the stated precondition.

## What this chapter argues, in one paragraph

Similarity detection is mature, and the best of it is honest about its limits:
it finds similarity, not plagiarism, and a human decides. But similarity
detection can only see collusion, because it compares submissions against each
other. Authorship attribution can in principle see outsourcing, but it is posed
as closed-set identification against a candidate list, which a course does not
have. AI-content detection exists but its accuracy depends on in-distribution
training data that a course does not have either. CodeGuard's contribution is
not a better algorithm in any one of these strands; it is the combination of
structural similarity with per-student stylistic baselining, scored into one
ranked, auditable figure with its evidence attached, and measured honestly
enough to state where it fails.

That is a defensible contribution for a final-year project, and it is defensible
precisely because it does not claim to beat Caliskan-Islam at their own task.

1. `novak2019` first and properly. It frames the chapter, supplies the thesis
   anchor, and its bibliography will close most of the gaps in the table above.
2. `zakeri2023` for the taxonomy and the honest limitations.
3. `caliskan2015` closely, with the caveat section above in mind while reading.
4. `schleimer2003` and `prechelt2002` for Strand 1; both are short.
5. `pawlik2016` only as far as needed to describe what APTED does. It is a
   dense algorithms paper and the report does not need its proofs.
6. `nguyen2023` last, and only for the Layer 3 justification.
